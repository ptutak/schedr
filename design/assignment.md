## Current

### Limits and mapped tasks

- **Concurrency limits use run and current DAG versions** (D87): `max_active_per_run` comes from
  each run’s own DAG version. Across-run `max_active_tasks` and `max_active` come from the current
  DAG version.
- **Mapped instances each count toward concurrency limits** (D89): Each mapped instance counts
  toward all three task concurrency limits. The whole-task `-1` row does not count.
- **Concurrency limits cannot be overridden through the API or CLI** (D95): Changing a concurrency
  limit without a code change has no API or CLI override. Limits shared across DAGs are a proposal
  for M6.

### Placement and ordering

- **The master keeps concurrency counts in memory** (D88): The master counts running, cleaning-up
  and assigned-not-started instances per DAG and task. It rebuilds counts from the database at
  startup and checks them on assignment.
- **Assignment stays oldest-ready-first; no priorities between DAGs** (D384): Eligible tasks are
  assigned oldest-ready-first. There are no task or DAG priorities and no fairness between DAGs.
- **A round releases the core lock between its batches of assignments** (D469): A round no longer
  holds the master's core lock from start to end: it lets go between its batches of assignments (and
  between its other parts), so a worker's turn waiting for the lock gets in between them. Each batch
  is still one transaction; what a batch decides is read again after the lock is taken back. With
  one worker, the round's hold is what a `Started` waits for (M5.1a: scenario 23's rounds up to 4.1
  s, a turn waiting up to 6.6 s). Not chosen: the round's hold left to M5.1d; M5.1d's set-based
  statements first.
- **Tasks past a round's assignment cap are not visited; they show as pending** (D481): Once a round
  has made its assignments (`ASSIGNMENTS_PER_ROUND`), it stops going through the ready tasks: the
  rest are counted as waiting for the next round (`tasks_waiting`, `next_round`) and their reason
  becomes a pending one the UI shows ("pending: waiting for a round with room ..."), at most 500 a
  round (one statement; a longer backlog over the next rounds), written with the round's other
  reasons, instead of each being checked for its limits and workers; a round that reaches them
  writes their own reason again. A wait step among them switched off or out of time still ends in
  that round. Not chosen: visiting every ready task each round (1,340 visits, 1.6 s of a round in
  stress 23).
- **The round's assignments set-based: wait-step checks once per round, claims and deadlines per
  batch** (D482): A round finds the ready wait steps that are switched off or past their `max_wait`
  with a query each for all of them, before assigning, instead of one or two queries per wait step
  at each visit; and it claims the round's batch of assignments (attempts, `try_number`, the
  instances' state) and keeps their deadlines in statements over the whole batch, as a turn's
  `Finished`s are written (decision 396). A batch that fails is still assigned one by one. Not
  chosen: the per-task statements (in stress 23, 10.8 s, 4.8 s and 1.5 s of 17.6 s of assignment).
- **Each tick one probe statement; every check on a stored row or column; triggers and events in a
  round of their own** (D485): Each master round starts with one statement, the probe
  (`Database::probe`): for each read the round makes, whether it would find anything. Inside
  `Database::in_round` a read whose check said no returns nothing; the probe looks a tick ahead, and
  is taken again after a write ends or for a time-bounded read past what it covered. Every check
  reads a stored row or column: a scheduled DAG stores `next_fire_at` (unknown after a switch
  change, a time come already while due runs wait or it is held), so a round visits the DAGs due,
  queued or marked and keeps nothing in memory to decide it; the tick stays 1 s. Triggers and events
  get a round of their own, with their moments stored (M5.1f2). Not chosen: rounds gated by change
  signals, a stretched idle tick, an in-memory model of the state.
